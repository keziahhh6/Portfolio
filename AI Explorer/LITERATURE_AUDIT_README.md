## 1. Topic Overview

The adoption of electronic wallets (e-wallets) in the Philippines has accelerated substantially over the past decade, with the COVID-19 pandemic serving as a structural inflection point that compressed years of projected digital payment adoption into a period of fewer than 24 months. Platforms such as GCash and Maya (formerly PayMaya) have emerged as dominant retail payment instruments, facilitating peer-to-peer transfers, merchant payments, government disbursements, and microfinance access for millions of previously unbanked or underbanked Filipinos. The Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) has institutionalized this trajectory through its Digital Payments Transformation Roadmap (DPTR) 2020–2023, targeting a 50 percent share of financial transactions conducted digitally by 2023.

Financial inclusion — defined by the BSP as the state in which all Filipinos have access to a wide range of financial services that are accessible, affordable, and responsive to their needs — remains an unfinished agenda. The 2021 BSP Financial Inclusion Survey reported that 56 percent of Filipino adults remained unbanked, though ownership of e-money accounts had increased markedly. This divergence between e-wallet penetration and formal banking inclusion raises substantive questions for policymakers, researchers, and development practitioners: Does e-wallet adoption constitute genuine financial inclusion, or does it represent a parallel informal digital economy that bypasses prudential regulation and long-term asset-building pathways?

Answering these questions requires robust, empirically grounded literature. However, the increasing integration of AI-assisted tools into academic research workflows introduces a category of methodological risk that has received insufficient attention in Philippine research practice: hallucination — the generation of plausible but fabricated citations, statistics, and institutional claims by large language models. This project demonstrates a replicable audit methodology for identifying, classifying, and correcting AI-generated research errors before they enter policy discourse or academic publication.

The adoption of e-wallets in the Philippines has been characterized by rapid growth, driven by increasing smartphone penetration, regulatory support from the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, and behavioral shifts accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Scholars and institutional researchers have identified several converging dynamics:

**F1.** According to the BSP Financial Inclusion Survey (2021), approximately 71 percent of Filipino adults reported owning or having access to an e-wallet account by the end of 2021, representing a doubling of digital payment participation from the 2019 baseline of 35 percent.

**F2.** A 2022 study published in the *Asian Journal of Finance and Accounting* by Reyes and Santos found that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness — core constructs of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) — were the primary predictors of GCash adoption among urban Filipino consumers, explaining 68 percent of adoption variance in their sample of 450 Metro Manila respondents.

**F3.** The World Bank's 2022 Global Findex Database reported that the Philippines achieved a formal financial inclusion rate of 78 percent by 2021, with e-money accounts accounting for the majority of the increase from the 2017 baseline.

**F4.** Research by Mendoza (2020), published in the *Philippine Journal of Development Studies*, found that digital payment adoption among women micro-entrepreneurs in Mindanao increased by 43 percent between 2018 and 2020, attributing growth primarily to the expansion of GCash merchant network infrastructure in secondary cities including Davao, Cagayan de Oro, and General Santos.

**F5.** The BSP's National Strategy for Financial Inclusion (NSFI) 2022–2028 explicitly mandates that all rural banks operating in Mindanao integrate QR Ph-compatible payment terminals by 2024, with non-compliance subject to BSP Circular 1154 penalties.

**F6.** A 2021 meta-analysis by Tan et al., published in the *Journal of Financial Technology and Innovation*, synthesized 34 studies across Southeast Asia and concluded that trust in digital financial systems — particularly concerns regarding data security and fraud — was the most significant barrier to e-wallet adoption in lower-income populations, with an average effect size of r = 0.61 across studies.

**F7.** The Philippine Institute for Development Studies (PIDS) issued a 2021 policy note (PIDS Discussion Paper No. 2021-18) concluding that while e-wallet penetration had expanded rapidly in urban centers, rural and agricultural communities — particularly in BARMM and Caraga — continued to face access gaps attributable to infrastructure deficits and low financial literacy, and recommending a targeted government subsidy program for community digital finance agents.

## 4. Bias and Reliability Assessment

### 4.1 Metro Manila Centrism

The most structurally pervasive bias in the AI-generated literature review is the implicit assumption that Philippine digital finance adoption patterns are homogeneous across geography. Five of the seven AI-generated findings (F1, F2, F3, F6, and the structural claim in F8) make no geographic distinction within the Philippines. The one finding that attempts regional specificity (F4, on Mindanao) is a fabricated citation. This reflects a bias embedded in the AI model's training data, which disproportionately represents Metro Manila-centric research, English-language publications, and urban consumer behavior studies. The practical consequence for policy is significant: if financial inclusion recommendations derived from Metro Manila TAM studies are applied to rural Mindanao markets — where infrastructure conditions, linguistic context, and MSME composition differ materially — interventions will be miscalibrated and resource allocation will be misguided.

### 4.2 Statistical Exaggeration

Two of the quantitative claims in the AI summary (F1 and F3) involved substantially inflated figures relative to verified primary sources. The AI reported 71 percent e-wallet account ownership where BSP data supports 29 percent; it reported 78 percent formal financial inclusion where World Bank Findex supports 51 percent. This pattern — in which AI systems generate statistics that are directionally consistent with the narrative (adoption is growing) but empirically overstated — is particularly dangerous in policy contexts because inflated baselines produce underestimates of the remaining inclusion gap, potentially reducing perceived urgency for intervention funding and program design.

### 4.3 Citation Fabrication

Three of the eight audited entries (F2, F4, F6) were fully fabricated citations. Each followed a structurally consistent hallucination pattern:

- **Plausible author surnames** drawn from common Filipino or Asian name pools
- **Credible-sounding journal titles** that mimic real publication naming conventions
- **Internally consistent methodological detail** (sample sizes, effect sizes, study counts) that lends false empirical authority
- **Substantive claims broadly consistent** with legitimate research trends, making the fabrications difficult to detect without source verification

This pattern confirms findings in the emerging literature on LLM hallucination in academic contexts: AI systems do not fabricate randomly, but rather generate citations that are locally coherent with the surrounding text, making them systematically deceptive to readers who do not verify sources.

### 4.4 Regulatory Misattribution

Finding F5 illustrates a distinct error category: selective accuracy combined with fabricated regulatory specificity. The AI correctly identified a real policy document (BSP NSFI 2022–2028) and a real regulatory instrument (BSP Circular 1154) but combined them into a compliance mandate that does not exist. This type of error is particularly consequential in regulatory analysis, where false compliance deadlines or misattributed penalties could mislead businesses, LGU compliance officers, or community organizations about their actual legal obligations.

### 4.5 Implications for Policy Recommendations

The cumulative effect of these bias categories — geographic overgeneralization, statistical exaggeration, citation fabrication, and regulatory misattribution — is that unverified AI-generated literature reviews, if incorporated into policy briefs or program designs, would systematically overestimate national adoption baselines, underestimate regional access gaps (particularly in Mindanao and BARMM), attribute recommendations to non-existent peer-reviewed evidence, and potentially misrepresent the regulatory environment. For LGU officers and development practitioners in Davao Region who rely on literature reviews to design MSME financial literacy programs, the practical stakes of unaudited AI outputs are not theoretical — they affect budget allocation, targeting decisions, and community trust.

## 5. Critical Reflection on AI Limitations

### The Productivity Gain and the Verification Imperative

Large language models have materially altered the economics of literature synthesis. A task that previously required a research associate several weeks — scanning databases, reading abstracts, extracting findings, organizing thematic clusters — can now be compressed into a matter of minutes with a well-constructed prompt. This is not a trivial productivity gain. For a university research center with limited staffing resources, as is common in Mindanao academic institutions, the ability to generate a structured thematic synthesis across dozens of papers in a single session has genuine value for the early stages of a research project. AI-assisted synthesis is particularly effective at identifying conceptual frameworks, naming relevant theoretical constructs (TAM, DOI theory, trust models), and structuring the logical architecture of a literature review before deep reading begins.

However, the audit conducted in this project demonstrates that this productivity gain is accompanied by a qualitatively different category of methodological risk than those traditionally associated with literature review errors. Human reviewers make mistakes of omission — they miss relevant papers, or they read a source superficially and mischaracterize its findings. These are errors of imperfect attention. AI systems make errors of confabulation — they generate sources, statistics, and regulatory details that are structurally indistinguishable from accurate information and that are embedded within narratively coherent text. The confabulation error is harder to detect precisely because the output reads well.

### The Specificity Paradox

The audit revealed a consistent pattern that may be called the specificity paradox: the more precise an AI-generated claim appears — specific sample sizes, effect sizes, discussion paper numbers, circular references — the more likely it is to contain a fabricated element. This inverts the intuitive heuristic that specificity signals reliability. In AI output, specificity is not evidence of verification; it is a stylistic feature of confident generation. A claim that "trust is a significant barrier to e-wallet adoption" is likely to be directionally accurate and safely attributed to a broad literature. A claim that "Tan et al. (2021) found an effect size of r = 0.61 in a meta-analysis of 34 Southeast Asian studies" is likely to be fabricated. Researchers who have internalized the standard academic norm — that specific citations are more rigorous than general claims — are particularly vulnerable to this inversion.

### The Regional Data Gap as a Structural Vulnerability

The Davao Region and Mindanao more broadly represent a structural vulnerability in AI-assisted Philippine research precisely because they are underrepresented in the English-language, indexed academic literature that constitutes most AI training corpora. When an AI system is asked to generate findings on Mindanao-specific digital finance adoption, it has insufficient verified data to draw from and compensates by extrapolating from Metro Manila studies, fabricating plausible-sounding regional research, or importing findings from comparable Southeast Asian markets. This is not a failure of the AI model per se; it is a reflection of a real gap in the published literature. The practical lesson for researchers in Davao Region is that AI-assisted literature synthesis is least reliable precisely where it would be most useful — in generating regionally specific evidence — and that local primary research remains irreplaceable.

### Lessons Learned

The audit process yielded three operational lessons for responsible AI-assisted research practice. First, statistical figures should be treated as provisional until verified against the primary source; the direction of AI statistical errors is systematically toward overstatement, not understatement, making this a predictable rather than random risk. Second, citations should be verified in Scopus or Google Scholar before the paper enters any draft; the cost of verification per citation is low, and the cost of a fabricated citation in a published policy brief is high. Third, the most professionally valuable skill in AI-assisted research is not prompt engineering per se, but the capacity to read AI output with calibrated skepticism — treating it as a capable but unreliable research assistant whose work requires systematic review before it enters the scholarly record.

## 7. Research Integrity Checklist

The following checklist documents the complete audit workflow applied in this project. It is designed to be replicable for any AI-assisted literature review in Philippine social science, development economics, or financial technology research.

---

### Phase 1: AI Output Generation

- [x] Research question and scope defined prior to AI prompting
- [x] System prompt constructed with explicit topic constraints (Philippine e-wallet adoption, financial inclusion, Mindanao relevance)
- [x] AI-generated literature review summary produced (GPT-4-class model)
- [x] Raw AI output preserved in unedited form for audit reference
- [x] Individual claims extracted and numbered (F1–F7, plus structural claims)

### Phase 2: Claim Extraction and Source Identification

- [x] All quantitative statistics identified and flagged for verification
- [x] All named citations (author, year, journal, title) extracted into audit log
- [x] All named regulatory instruments and policy documents extracted
- [x] Verification sources identified for each claim category (BSP, World Bank, PIDS, Scopus)

### Phase 3: Verification

- [x] BSP Financial Inclusion Survey 2021 consulted for e-wallet account ownership figures
- [x] World Bank Global Findex 2021 consulted for Philippines formal account ownership rate
- [x] Scopus and Google Scholar searched for each named journal citation
- [x] BSP Circular registry consulted for regulatory citation accuracy
- [x] PIDS Discussion Paper archive consulted for discussion paper number verification
- [x] Journal title legitimacy checked against ABDC and Scopus journal lists where applicable

### Phase 4: Classification and Correction

- [x] Each claim classified as VERIFIED, PARTIALLY VERIFIED, or HALLUCINATION
- [x] Hallucination type identified for each failed entry (fabricated citation, inflated statistic, misattributed regulatory detail, Metro Manila overgeneralization)
- [x] Corrections documented with replacement figures or source recommendations
- [x] Partially verified entries flagged with specific verification actions required

### Phase 5: Bias Assessment

- [x] Geographic bias assessed (Metro Manila centrism vs. Mindanao/regional specificity)
- [x] Statistical directionality assessed (overstatement vs. understatement patterns)
- [x] Citation fabrication pattern characterized (plausible-surname + credible journal-name pattern)
- [x] Regulatory misattribution pattern documented
- [x] Policy implications of uncorrected bias assessed
